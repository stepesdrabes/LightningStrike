Striker 1.0: drum detection benchmarks

Striker is the model that decides which kick, snare and hi-hat hits a song actually contains, so LightningStrike can light a room in time with the drummer. It is measured here against the best published result on every public drum transcription benchmark that has a comparable number. Every score comes from a model that never trained on the dataset it is scored on.

How a song is processed

  1. Separate the drums. HTDemucs (168 MB) pulls a drum stem out of the mix.
  2. Split the kit. MDX23C DrumSep (438 MB) turns that stem into kick, snare, hi-hat and cymbal tracks.
  3. Transcribe six views. ADTOF, a recurrent neural net (1.8 MB), transcribes the original mix, the drum stem and each of the four separated tracks.
  4. Decide. Striker 1.0 (6.5 MB) takes every candidate hit those views propose, plus the attacks in the separated audio, measures 85 things about each one, and keeps the ones it believes. It is a gradient-boosted tree ensemble, five models per instrument averaged, trained with LightGBM.
  5. Light the room. Kick, snare and hi-hat streams reach the lighting engine; ride and crash hits join the hi-hat stream, and each hit carries a loudness taken from its own separated source.

Without the model the app falls back to rule-based detection, so a machine missing the weights still runs.

Two label conventions had to be excluded rather than learned. RBMA13's public snare class leaves claps and side sticks out, and STAR's backing stems hide unlabelled clap residue. Trained on either, the model learned to reject claps and snaps, which a lighting app needs to catch, so neither teaches snares.

How it is scored

Five instrument classes where the dataset labels five (kick, snare, hi-hat, cymbal, tom), kick, snare and hi-hat otherwise. A detected hit counts when it falls within 50 ms of a labelled one, F is summed over every onset of the scored classes, and the protocol follows ADTOF and Vogl et al. so the numbers line up with theirs. Thresholds are chosen on the other datasets, never on the one being scored.

Where it is not ahead

  • Same-dataset protocols. Papers that train and test on the same dataset, with the same drummers, kits and rooms in both, still report more: 0.988 on IDMT and 0.929 on ENST drums (Southall et al. 2017), and 0.900 on accompanied ENST (Khanal and Lee 2026). Trained the same way, Striker reaches 0.987 and 0.924.
  • Tighter timing. With a 20 ms window instead of 50 ms, IDMT drops to 0.917 against Vogl et al.'s 0.952. Hi-hat onsets carry the largest timing error. At that window it still leads on RBMA13, 0.702 against 0.673.
  • Not comparable. RBMA13's five-class labels are not public, and MDBDrums++ has no stated protocol behind its one published number.

Known weaknesses

  • Toms are weak, 0.45 to 0.72. They are not used for lighting.
  • RBMA13 snares stay near 0.65, partly because its labels leave claps out.
  • Quiet ghost notes and dense electronic percussion remain the main source of error.
  • On Groove MIDI most misses are notes played below MIDI velocity 40.

What it costs

Accuracy here is bought with computation. Two neural separations and six transcription passes run before Striker decides anything. A 2 minute 27 second song takes about 31 seconds on a Radeon GPU through DirectML, or 202 seconds on a Ryzen 5 3600 with a 6.3 GB peak. Re-analysing the same song from cached separation takes 11 seconds. This is preparation done once per song, not real-time detection.

In the app

Across the 39 songs both versions cover, Striker emits 4% fewer kicks, 24% fewer snares and 21% fewer hi-hat and cymbal hits than the rule-based detection it replaced: fewer hits, and more of them real. On hand-marked passages it finds 18 of 19 confirmed snares, and of five hits marked wrong before, it still plays one where the old version played four. Marked missed hits are the part that did not improve: 22 of 37 found, against 23.
