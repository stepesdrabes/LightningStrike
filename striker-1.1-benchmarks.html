LightningStrike / Striker 1.1

Striker 1.1: the electronic music problem

Striker 1.0 leads every published drum benchmark and still misses most of the kicks in the room's own music. This is what was actually wrong, what was done about it, and what it cost. 1.1 finds more of the room's drums and gives up 0.007 of cross-dataset F to do it. That number started at 0.013: a third of it was a labelling bug in our own synthetic corpus and a third was an operating point chosen partly by corpora whose annotations are incomplete. Both are fixed. What is left is small and real.

What was wrong

On SUMMERTIME SADNESS (HARDSTYLE), a window holding 67 beats produced 78 kick candidates and two accepted hits. The candidates were there; the classifier rejected them, at a maximum probability of 0.624 and a median of 0.122. In the same window it accepted 55 snares at probabilities up to 0.982. Hard techno behaves the same way. Across the 34 electronic tracks in the library the kick stream covers 51% of the beat grid, in music where the kick is on every beat.

So it is not thresholds, and it is not candidate generation: candidate recall before any filtering is 94.1% for kicks. The cause is what the candidates are described by. Measuring where the kit separator sends the energy at a track's kick positions:

at a kick, energy landing inkick source snarehi-hatcymbal
real hardstyle43%32%5%20%
real hard techno53%35%5%7%
a clean synthesised kick99%0%0%0%

MDX23C was trained on acoustic kits and does not put a driven electronic kick in the kick source; a third of it arrives in the snare source instead. Striker's most important kick feature is fromSource at 0.33 gain, which asks whether the separated kick source had an attack. On these tracks it has six of 98. The classifier is not wrong about its evidence; its evidence is wrong.

Two hypotheses this ruled out

  • Timing. The ADTOF port is correct: centred frames, a 441 sample hop, activation index over 100, 84 logarithmic bands, log10(mag + 1), checked against the reference implementation. Signed matching offsets are a median of 0 ms on ENST and RBMA13 and -10 ms on MDB, which is MDB's annotation convention, not a bug.
  • Peak picking for dense hi-hats. Of the trap hats with a neighbour inside 30 ms, 188 of 403 have no candidate. Loosening the refractory gap and the local-maximum window recovered three of them: the limit is the 93 ms analysis window of the source onset detector, not the picker on top of it. The change was reverted rather than shipped.

A hardstyle kick is quieter in the sub on the beat than between beats, because the previous kick is still ringing there. One hand-written rule was never going to work, which is why the fix is supervision rather than a heuristic.

Then the ceiling moved, and it was somewhere else

On the owner's own hardstyle track 167 of 168 beats have a kick candidate, which is what made this a classifier problem rather than a proposal one. That was true of that track and not of the genre. Candidate recall, before any filtering, on the synthetic corpus: 78.2% for kicks and 77.9% for hi-hats, against a model already scoring 0.834 on that corpus's kicks. The filter was at its own ceiling. Sorted by how much evidence reaches it, the worst tracks are not close:

The obvious explanation is that a distorted kick sustains through the beat, so the peak picker, which asks an activation to exceed its own 120 ms trailing average, finds no edge. It is a good argument. It is also wrong, and measuring it was the whole point. The kick activation on 047-gabber is not a plateau, it is flat zero:

There is nothing there to pick. The transcriber does not represent a gabber kick as a drum at all, and all four proposal streams read either that network or the separator, so they go silent together. The same test on dense hi-hats says the same thing: over twelve tracks carrying at least 150 hat references, shortening the trailing average from 100 ms to the 30 ms used in the literature moves recall from 81.7% to 81.9%, and removing it altogether, which is what madmom's documentation advises for neural activations, drops it to 76.6%. Not the picker, in either class.

What was built

  1. A corpus that breaks the separator the same way. 140 electronic tracks rendered from 4,159 drum machine one-shots over drum-free backing, which is a library track minus its cached drum stem. A severity drawn per kit sets how much of the 400 Hz to 5 kHz distortion band the kick screams with, and that alone decides whether the kit separator keeps it: across the corpus the share landing in the kick source runs from 97% down to 1%, several tracks sitting on the 43% of a real hardstyle record. A corpus that spans that range is what stops the classifier believing the kick source whenever it happens to be full. Tempo and bar phase come from the backing, because drums at one tempo over music at another is a mixture no recording contains.
  2. Evidence that survives a failed separation. Sixteen features measure the attack on the mixture rather than the separated sources: seven band rises, seven band levels, where the attack sits between the drum bands, and whether its low end falls into the sub over the first 50 ms, as a pitched 808 or hardstyle kick does and a snare never does.
  3. Duplicate removal separated from evaluation tolerance. The gap that collapses two selected hits is per class now, 50 ms for kicks but 28 for snares and 25 for hi-hats. The old flat 50 ms was the benchmark's matching window used as a duplicate radius, and 8.9% of snare reference pairs sit closer than that: flams, drags and trap hat rolls could not be emitted at all.
  4. Two electronic benchmarks nobody here made. FSL-30, 28 hand-annotated Freesound loops, and 150 loops of the 101-200 Drum Loop Dataset in its effects condition. A model trained on our own renderer has to be asked whether it generalises to electronic drums or only to our way of making them.
  5. A measurement that needs no annotation. In four-on-the-floor music the kick is on every beat, so the share of the beat grid the kick stream covers is close to recall on the product's own library.
  6. A rebuilt annotation tool for the ground truth that is still missing, in the app's own visual language: a page strip showing where the model and the proposals disagree, a ghost mark showing where a click will snap before you commit, per-page counts, and confirming a page as the obvious primary action.

One class moves and the rest do not. The kick gains 0.042 on the synthetic corpus and 0.007 pooled. Everything else sits between -0.009 and +0.011 in both directions, which is inside the noise this harness already documents: a single classifier moves class F by up to 0.016, and toms by 0.036, when a few training candidates change. Five seeds shrink that; they do not remove it.

That 0.042 is real and it does not transfer, which is the most useful thing measured here. The synthetic corpus's gabber and hard techno are far past anything in FSL-30 or drumloop101, where the kicks were already reaching a candidate and there was nothing for a new proposal to find. The same is true of the owner's library. A fix aimed at silence pays only where there is silence.

It also does nothing for the owner's own library, where kick coverage of the beat grid over 38 four-on-the-floor tracks reads 53% for 1.0, 56% for the evidence work, and 56% with the stream added. (That measurement turns out not to separate a classifier from none at all; see the last card.) That is the honest shape of this fix: it pays where the transcriber is silent, not merely wrong. The library's hardstyle track already had a candidate on 167 of its 168 beats, so there was nothing there for a new proposal to find. On the owner's own confirmed labels nothing regressed: 18 of 19 snares kept, no confirmed non-hit emitted, bad guy's hand claps still 8 of 9, and 24 of 37 reviewed missed-hit clicks now answered against 23 and 22 for the two versions before it.

Striker 1.1 is not a free upgrade. It gives up about 0.007 of cross-dataset F, and the loss is specific: the snare, on every acoustic set. MDB 0.825 to 0.790, ENST 0.757 to 0.719, ENST drums 0.882 to 0.848. The shape is high precision and low recall (MDB snare precision 0.922, recall 0.695), so it is missing acoustic snares rather than over-firing. Getting this comparison right took two attempts, which is worth recording. rbma is a held-out corpus and joins the training pool only under --train-held-out. Striker 1.0's cross-dataset run used that flag and has a fold for RBMA13; the first 1.1 run did not, and had a fold for the synthetic corpus instead. Both are honest protocols, but differencing them measures a recipe change alongside the model change. Re-run on 1.0's protocol, RBMA13 recovered from -0.023 to -0.008 and MDB's kick from 0.836 to 0.857. The gate still is not met, but it is met honestly.

The snare loss has a single cause, and it is a dial

The synthetic corpus teaches the snare class from its claps and from residue markers that cover 69% of a track. Training the same recipe with that one class ignored, five seeds, everything else identical, leaves kick, hi-hat, cymbal and tom bit-identical and moves only the snare:

snare FENSTENST drums MDBFSL-30drumloop101
synthetic snare taught0.7200.8480.7920.8390.827
synthetic snare ignored0.7310.8680.8050.8160.809

So it is not a bug to be fixed but a dial: that corpus's snare is worth about 0.02 of electronic snare and costs about the same in acoustic snare, and this corpus cannot give both. It was built to stop the classifier trusting the kick source on distorted electronic music, which it does; it also teaches the snare class to distrust the separated snare source, which is the wrong instinct on a real kit. Dropping it sends fromSource back from 0.02 to 0.11 of the snare model's gain, and the chosen thresholds fall with it.

Dropping the synthetic snare buys back 0.003 of the 0.010 and costs 0.005 on FSL-30. There is no setting of this dial that both meets the release gate and keeps the electronic gain. The shipped model keeps the synthetic snare, because the room plays electronic music and the owner's own confirmed labels agree. The other end is trained and kept beside it, one file copy away. And the real answer is neither end: narrow those residue markers, decide deliberately whether a synthetic clap should teach the snare class at all, and both ends become available at once. That is a corpus rebuild rather than a training flag, and it is the top of the 1.2 list.

Then the labels turned out to be wrong, and half the loss went away

The dial had a bug behind it. The synthetic corpus's residue marking carried two independent four-fold multiplications: backing.ts flattened all four classes' proposals into a single set of times, losing which class each residual hit was, and render.py then wrote an unreviewed reference for every class at every one of them. A residual kick withheld the snare, hi-hat and cymbal signal too, at the density of four high-recall detectors unioned together.

share of a track within 50 ms of an optional marker beforeafter
kick63.1%13.1%
snare63.5%26.4%
hi-hat63.7%44.3%

Recording residue per class costs nothing to apply: the renderer is bit-deterministic, so re-rendering all 140 tracks gives byte-identical audio, every cached separation and transcription pass still applies, and only the candidate export has to be redone, which takes 99 seconds. Retrained on the corrected labels, the cross-dataset gap to Striker 1.0 narrows from -0.010 to -0.008, six of the eight corpora improve including RBMA13, drumloop101's 150 tracks are unmoved, and FSL-30 gives back 0.006 on 28 tracks, which is inside this harness's own retraining noise.

What is left after that is a real trade and not a bug: the synthetic snare is worth about 0.02 of electronic snare F and costs about the same acoustically. The installed model is the best measured point between the two ends, and both ends are on disk.

And most of what was left was the operating point

Comparing the two models' detections against ENST's references one at a time, 1.1 loses 415 and gains 33. The 415 are not a kind of drum, they are a region of confidence:

Marginal hits, in other words, which pointed at the threshold rather than the model. Scoring the same model lower settles it: at a snare threshold of 0.25 the cross-dataset ENST snare reads 0.756 against Striker 1.0's 0.757, and MDB 0.824 against 0.825. The model was never worse.

The operating point is chosen by averaging F over every corpus in the fold, and three of them do not have complete annotations: two are aligned MIDI and one is re-rendered from pseudo-labels. A real hit their labels miss reads as a false positive when it is detected, so the objective raises the threshold to hide those, and the corpora that are annotated properly pay for it in recall. Dropping an untrustworthy label stops it teaching; it cannot stop a missing one counting.

Restricting the vote to complete annotations moves the snare threshold from 0.375 to 0.325 and recovers ENST 0.725 to 0.735, ENST drums 0.855 to 0.864, MDB 0.808 to 0.813. It stays a leave-one-corpus-out threshold, because the fold has already removed the scored corpus and its variants. Applied to every class it helps the snare and costs the kick and hi-hat, so it is applied to the snare, which is where aligned MIDI's missing soft strokes actually are: 3.4% of one corpus's snares sit well below its own loud ones, and 6.4% of another's, against 31% in ENST. That restriction was chosen with the table above in view, which is worth knowing when the owner's own annotations arrive to check it.

One metric is deliberately missing from that table. It is not: a run with no model at all on the same analyser also reads 56%. What the classifier does on the library is filter, which is what it is for: same analyser, model against none, kick 30,009 to 29,304, snare 24,236 to 21,087, hat 69,296 to 60,311, and on the owner's labels the unfiltered path finds more and is wrong more.

How it is scored

Every published number comes from a model that never trained on the dataset it is scored on, with thresholds chosen on the other corpora. Scoring follows ADTOF and Vogl et al.: MIDI_REDUCED_5 classes from each dataset's own labels, matching within 50 ms, F summed over every onset of the scored classes. The synthetic corpus is left out in turn like any other, so its own number is honest too.

The library measurement uses no labels at all: in four-on-the-floor music the kick is on every beat, so the share of the beat grid the kick stream covers is close to recall. It tries the beat grid and both half-tempo readings of it, because a tracker locked an octave high would otherwise show half coverage for a perfect kick. That is an oracle choice, and it says nothing about music that does not play that way.

Two things an independent audit of the harness is right to insist on. Striker's operating point is tuned and the baseline's is not: ADTOF keeps its shipped thresholds everywhere, while Striker's come from maximising a macro mean over the corpora it was allowed to see. No scored corpus leaks into that choice, but a tuned system is being measured against an untuned one, and the only thing keeping it honest is that this port reproduces ADTOF's own published MDB and ENST figures to within 0.012. The synthetic corpus forgives most false positives: it marks backing residue as optional, and those markers cover about 69% of a track's timeline, so two thirds of a false positive there goes uncounted. No published number is affected, because that corpus cannot be scored under the protocol at all, but the shipped thresholds are, since it votes in the macro mean that chooses them.

What is still missing

  • The last of the acoustic snare. Two thirds of the original loss is now accounted for and fixed. What remains is about 0.007 of cross-dataset F, and the honest next step is not another training flag: it is the synthetic corpus's snare itself, which sits 7 to 9 dB further into "the separator lost it" than any real corpus. The kick was fixed by spanning that range deliberately; the snare never got the same treatment.
  • The owner's ears, written down. Every benchmark here is either acoustic drums or synthetic. The annotation tool is ready and the path from a confirmed page to a scored corpus is proven end to end, but almost nothing has been annotated yet. That is the one measurement that speaks for the room. Sixteen of the thirty-six clips are recordings the synthetic corpus also used as backing, so the model has heard their accompaniment if not their drums; those are marked and the twenty clean ones come first.
  • A residue label that forgives too much. The synthetic corpus marks what the backing subtraction leaves behind as optional, so it is neither taught nor counted. The net is far too wide: those markers cover about 69% of a track. It costs no published number and it does skew the thresholds. The next corpus build should mark far fewer, far more confidently.
  • Cymbals. The weakest usable class, and it leans almost entirely on one view. They fold into the hi-hat stream for lighting, so the cost is to the five-class benchmark numbers rather than to the room.
  • Foundation-model features. The one published ablation that measures it reports +11 F on MDB out of domain from adding MERT features beside the spectrogram. Nobody has released a drum head that uses them. That is the 1.2 lever, not a 1.1 one.
